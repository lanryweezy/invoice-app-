Here is a scenario that happens every day in Nigerian business: A client says, "We want to buy your software. Send an invoice." The vendor immediately issues an invoice for ₦5,000,000. Three weeks later, the vendor asks for payment, only to hear, "Oh, procurement hasn't issued a Purchase Order (PO) yet, so the invoice is invalid."

The vendor is frustrated, cashflow is stalled, and trust is broken. This friction stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of financial operations: treating a Purchase Order and an Invoice as the same thing.

If you are scaling a business in Nigeria—whether you run an agency, a B2B SaaS, or a consulting firm—you cannot afford to mix these up. In corporate procurement, a PO protects the buyer, while an invoice gets the seller paid. Let’s break down exactly how they differ and how to build a foolproof financial operating system for your business.

## The Core Difference: Intent vs. Obligation

To put it simply: a Purchase Order is a request to buy; an Invoice is a demand for payment.

**The Purchase Order (PO):** The buyer generates this document before the work begins. It outlines exactly what they want to purchase, the agreed price, and the terms. When you, as the vendor, accept the PO, it becomes a legally binding contract. It means the buyer's finance team has officially approved the budget.

**The Invoice:** The seller (you) generates this document after the product is delivered or the milestone is reached. It references the PO number and officially requests the transfer of funds. No PO, no budget. No invoice, no cash.

## Why Nigerian SMEs Struggle with POs and Invoices

The Nigerian SME sector often operates informally. Business is done over WhatsApp, agreements are verbal, and trust is assumed. While this agility helps close deals fast, it falls apart when you start dealing with larger corporations, government agencies, or structured startups.

When an SME skips the PO stage and jumps straight to an invoice, they take on massive risk. You might deliver a month of consulting work, only to discover the person who hired you didn't have the internal budget authorization. The result? You join the infamous "I will sort you later" queue.

## The Ideal Workflow for B2B Transactions

To eliminate payment delays and look like a premium operator, adopt this exact workflow:

1. **The Quotation/Proposal:** You send a breakdown of costs.
2. **The Purchase Order:** The client's procurement team sends you a PO confirming they agree to the quote and have allocated the funds.
3. **The Delivery:** You deliver the goods or services as outlined in the PO.
4. **The Invoice:** You send a professional invoice that clearly states the PO number. (A missing PO number is the #1 reason corporate invoices are delayed).

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What comes first, the purchase order or the invoice?
The purchase order (PO) always comes first. The buyer issues the PO to request goods or services and confirm the budget. The seller issues the invoice later to request payment for those delivered goods.

### Can I send an invoice without a purchase order?
While you can, it's highly risky when dealing with corporate clients. Without a PO, there is no official proof that the buyer's company authorized the purchase, which often leads to unpaid invoices.

### Is a purchase order a legally binding contract?
Yes. Once a vendor accepts a purchase order from a buyer, it forms a legally binding contract detailing the goods, prices, and terms of delivery.
